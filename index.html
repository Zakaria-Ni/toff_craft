At their core, Toff graph games are finite, perfect-information games played by two players who alternate moves or one player depending on what game you are playing. The game state is defined by a graph—consisting of a set of vertices connected by edges—and a set of tokens or markers placed on these vertices. Depending on the specific ruleset of the variation, a valid move involves moving a token along an edge to an adjacent vertex, removing a vertex from the graph, or coloring a portion of the network. The most common iteration follows normal play convention, where the last player to make a legal move wins, and a player who cannot move loses. Because the available moves depend solely on the current state of the graph and not on which player's turn it is, Toff graph games are classified as impartial games. This classification allows mathematicians to analyze them using the Sprague-Grundy theorem, assigns a "Grundy value" or "nim-value" to each graph configuration, and reduces complex geometric or structural states to a single numeric equivalent. The strategy of a Toff graph game is inextricably linked to the topology of the underlying network. On simple structures, such as paths, cycles, or trees, calculating winning strategies is relatively straightforward.
